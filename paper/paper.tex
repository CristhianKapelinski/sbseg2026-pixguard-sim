\title{\sysname: A Deadline-Aware Testbed for Pix Fraud Detectors}

\author{Anonymized for double-blind review\inst{1}}

\address{Anonymized Institution}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Brazilian regulation now requires Pix, the instant-payment system used by about
170 million people, to block fraud before money settles, yet open fraud
benchmarks score detectors only in batch and ignore that pre-transaction
decision window. \sysname{} is an open testbed that scores any Pix fraud
detector by the \emph{pre-deadline flag fraction}, the share of frauds flagged
within a configurable decision deadline, alongside precision, recall, and
PR-AUC with 95\% confidence intervals. The metric ranks detectors that batch
metrics rank as equal: two with indistinguishable batch F1 flag
\textbf{\rfPre} versus \textbf{\gbPreThousand} of frauds within a 1000\,ms
deadline, an inversion that reappears on the released pix-fraud-br set
(\textbf{\PfbXgbPre} vs \textbf{\PfbGbPre}). Across three
independently-authored generators the numbers stay sub-perfect and spread out
(best PR-AUC \textbf{\PfbXgbPRAUC} on pix-fraud-br, \textbf{\TideHiXgbPRAUC} on
rare-illicit Tide), and detectors trained on single-hop fraud collapse on
multi-hop and coercion scenarios (recall \textbf{\eCoerc} and \textbf{\eMed}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Pix, the instant-payment system launched by the Central Bank of Brazil (BCB) in
2020, became the country's dominant payment method within five years: it moved
\textbf{63.8 billion transactions worth R\$ 26.9 trillion in
2024},\footnote{\href{https://portal.febraban.org.br/noticia/4290/pt-br/}{Pix foi o meio de pagamento mais usado no Brasil em 2024, Febraban, abr.\ 2025}.}
accounts for \textbf{54.7\% of all payment transactions} in Brazil (second half
of 2025),\footnote{\href{https://istoedinheiro.com.br/pix-ja-representa-547-das-transacoes-financeiras-do-pais-mostra-bc}{Pix j\'a representa 54,7\% das transa\c{c}\~oes financeiras do pa\'is, mostra BC, IstoÉ Dinheiro, abr.\ 2026}.}
and reaches roughly 170 million people through about 890 million registered
keys.\footnote{\href{https://www.bcb.gov.br/estabilidadefinanceira/estatisticaspix}{Estat\'isticas do Pix, Banco Central do Brasil}.}
A \emph{key} here is a DICT (Diret\'orio de Identificadores de Contas
Transacionais) alias, the directory entry that resolves a phone number, tax id,
or random token to a destination account. The same property that drives this
adoption, irrevocable real-time settlement, is what turns a scam into an
unrecoverable loss: once funds settle, recovery depends on tracing them through
downstream accounts before they are dispersed.

That risk is now material. Fraud refunds that could not be returned, because
the receiving account was emptied or closed, reached \textbf{R\$ 4.94 billion in
2024, up 70\% year over year}, across 3.45 million rejected refund
requests.\footnote{\href{https://www.infomoney.com.br/minhas-financas/prejuizo-com-fraudes-no-pix-cresce-70-e-atinge-r-49-bi-em-2024-segundo-bc/}{Preju\'izo com fraudes no Pix cresce 70\% e atinge R\$ 4,9 bi em 2024, segundo o BC, InfoMoney, abr.\ 2025}.}
A recent taxonomy of Pix fraud, built from a literature review and
banking-sector interviews, classifies fifteen scam methodologies into four
thematic groups (social engineering by authority/trust; by refund/benefit/
urgency; attacks with physical interaction; and software- and
remote-access attacks) and explicitly recommends building fraud simulators for
controlled testing~\cite{pizzolato2025taxonomy}. The regulatory response has
shifted from reactive refunds toward real-time prevention. Under the MED-2.0
mechanism (Mecanismo Especial de Devolu\c{c}\~ao, the BCB special refund
mechanism for fraud victims), governed by Resolu\c{c}\~ao BCB 493/2025 and
mandatory for all Pix participants from \textbf{2 February
2026},\footnote{\href{https://www.bcb.gov.br/content/estabilidadefinanceira/pix/MED/MED_2-0_Circuito_Pix-Dia_1.pdf}{Mecanismo Especial de Devolu\c{c}\~ao (MED 2.0), Banco Central do Brasil, 2026}.}
institutions trace and block diverted funds across \textbf{up to five subsequent
account layers} and freeze suspect amounts as a fraud alert propagates through
the receiving chain; the BCB estimates this can raise the recovery rate from
under 7\% to as much as 80\%.\footnote{\href{https://www.matera.com/br/blog/med-mecanismo-especial-de-devolucao/}{MED 2.0: guia do Mecanismo Especial de Devolu\c{c}\~ao para institui\c{c}\~oes, Matera, 2026}.}
No regulation fixes a latency in seconds: the mandate is to block as soon as
fraud is identified, so the decision deadline is a parameter, not a legal
number.

This raises a sharp question: how should we measure a Pix fraud detector when
what the regulation demands is not just accuracy but a decision \emph{in time}?
Conventional precision and recall are computed in batch, after the fact, and
say nothing about whether a flag would have arrived before the irrevocable
decision point. The closest open artifacts do not close this gap. General
anti-money-laundering (AML) simulators model laundering topology rather than
consumer-scam dynamics and are jurisdiction-agnostic; PaySim, a mobile-money
simulator, measures time in coarse hourly steps with no real-time
deadline~\cite{lopezrojas2016paysim}; Tide adds timestamped multi-hop laundering
chains but no instant-payment semantics, no MED, and no latency
metric~\cite{vandenbeukel2026tide}. The only open Pix-native generator,
pix-fraud-br, models just the first transfer to a single mule and reports only
batch metrics.\footnote{\href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/andremessina/pix-fraud-br}{pix-fraud-br: synthetic Pix fraud dataset (2{,}000{,}000 transactions), Hugging Face, 2026}.}
None scores detectors against the pre-transaction decision window the
regulation now centres on.

We close this gap with \sysname, a thin, detector- and generator-agnostic
evaluation harness that scores any Pix fraud detector by the
\emph{pre-deadline flag fraction}, the fraction of true frauds flagged within a
configurable pre-transaction decision deadline, in addition to conventional
metrics reported with 95\% confidence intervals. The payoff is immediate and
holds on real third-party data: two detectors with statistically
indistinguishable batch F1 are separated decisively by the deadline metric,
one catching \rfPre{} of frauds within a 1000\,ms window and the other
\gbPreThousand{}, and the \emph{same} inversion reappears on the released
pix-fraud-br set (\PfbXgbPre{} vs \PfbGbPre{}). \sysname{} consumes the output of
existing generators rather than competing with them; we validate it across
three independently-authored generators on identical detector inputs, where the
numbers stay imperfect and spread out, never a uniform 1.00.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{C1.} A deadline-aware evaluation metric, the pre-deadline flag
fraction, tied to the Pix real-time-blocking intent, with evidence on the
in-repo layer and on the real pix-fraud-br set that it ranks detectors that
batch precision and recall cannot (Sections~\ref{sec:method},~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{C2.} Reference definitions of two Pix-native scenarios absent
from open prior work, multi-hop MED-2.0 refund tracing and coercion, mapped onto
the published taxonomy, with the finding that single-hop-trained
detectors collapse on them (Sections~\ref{sec:method},~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{C3.} A cross-generator, checksum-pinned evaluation on three
independently-authored generators (in-repo, the released Tide HI/LI sets, and
the released pix-fraud-br set), reproducing the prior-art baseline within
tolerance and including a graph-neural-network baseline trained on GPU, released
as a seed-pinned artifact (Sections~\ref{sec:results},~\ref{sec:concl}).
\end{itemize}

it does not generate yet another dataset but consumes existing generators and
adds the deadline metric and the two missing scenarios, the empty column in

\section{Design and Method}\label{sec:method}

\noindent\textbf{Event schema.} Every generator maps into one normalized
Pix-native event record. Each record carries a relative timeline (the moment the
transfer is initiated and the moment settlement becomes irrevocable), a MED-layer
index for refund chains, and Pix-native signals: the DICT key in use, a device
change, a new payee, recent payer velocity, a remote-access session, and a
coercion indicator. A detector sees only the numeric feature columns and the
timeline; the label, the scenario name, and account identifiers are never
exposed to it. A fixed seed reproduces the in-repo stream exactly, so any run
yields the same events.

\noindent\textbf{Three independent generators.} The harness scores detectors on
three independently-authored sources through thin adapters, so a reported number
is never read off data the harness itself produced. (i)~An in-repo synthetic
generator builds a scale-free base interaction graph (a Barab\'asi--Albert graph
under a fixed seed) and emits the four Pix-native scenarios; it is the only
source that carries coercion and multi-hop MED-2.0, so it supplies the scenarios
rather than the external credibility. (ii)~The released Tide HI/LI AML
datasets~\cite{vandenbeukel2026tide}, pinned by the provider's checksum on
Zenodo, supply multi-hop laundering at extreme rarity (\TideHiRate{} and
\TideLiRate{} illicit). (iii)~The released pix-fraud-br set of 2{,}000{,}000
Pix-framed transactions supplies a Pix-native, single-hop generator whose
prior-art baseline we reproduce. Each input file is pinned by content checksum
and source URL. All data is synthetic and ground-truth labeled; no number here is
a real-world fraud rate.

\noindent\textbf{Scenarios.} \sysname{} instantiates four scenarios mapped onto
the taxonomy's four groups~\cite{pizzolato2025taxonomy}: \emph{account-takeover}
(remote-access ``ghost hand''), \emph{coercion} (the physical-interaction group:
robbery, express kidnapping, extortion), \emph{fake-MED refund} (the
refund/urgency group, extended to multi-hop MED-2.0 tracing of up to five
layers), and \emph{mule-chain} dispersal. Coercion is deliberately hard to
detect: the victim uses their own device in a genuine session, so the event
shows no device change and no remote flag, only a coercion indicator, an unusual
amount, and a new payee. A multi-hop MED chain emits one event per refund layer
with decaying signal strength and inter-hop delays, which lets the harness break
recall down by MED layer.

\noindent\textbf{The deadline metric.} Each detector returns a fraud score and
the decision latency it would incur, measured from the moment the transfer is
initiated. The harness trains each detector on a deterministic stratified split
and reports batch precision, recall, F1, and PR-AUC, plus the
\emph{pre-deadline flag fraction}: among true-fraud events, the share the
detector both flags (score at or above its threshold) and decides on within the
deadline window. The deadline window is the time the institution has to block a
transfer before settlement becomes irrevocable. A fraud flagged only after that
window is not actionable for pre-transaction blocking and does not count. The
metric is swept over a range of deadlines because the regulation fixes none.
Every proportion (recall, pre-deadline fraction, per-scenario and per-layer
recall) carries a Wilson 95\% confidence interval; F1 and PR-AUC carry a
seed-pinned bootstrap 95\% interval over 1{,}000 resamples.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:results}

measures the deadline metric on the in-repo layer; E2 tests detectors trained
only on single-hop fraud against the unseen scenarios; E3 and E5 evaluate the

The headline result is \textbf{\rfPre{} versus \gbPreThousand}. As
Table~\ref{tab:e1} and Figure~\ref{fig:results}(a) show, on the in-repo layer
the fast random forest (RF) and the slow gradient-boosting model (GB-slow) are
statistically indistinguishable in batch terms (F1 \rfF{} vs \gbF; PR-AUC
\rfPRAUC{} vs \gbPRAUC), yet the deadline metric separates them completely: at a
1000\,ms deadline RF flags \rfPre{}~[\rfPreLo,~\rfPreHi] of frauds in time while
GB-slow, whose 5000\,ms inference budget exceeds the window, flags
\gbPreThousand{}~[\gbPreThousand,~\gbPreThousandHi], recovering to \gbPreFiveK{}
only once the deadline reaches its budget. This is a latency property: a
detector cannot decide before its own budget elapses, so a batch metric is blind
to it. The same inversion appears on the real pix-fraud-br set, where the fast
XGBoost model (XGB) and GB-slow have near-equal batch F1 (\PfbXgbF{} vs \PfbGbF)
but pre-deadline fractions of \PfbXgbPre{} and \PfbGbPre{} at 1000\,ms. The
metric carries information that precision and recall do not, on synthetic and on
real third-party data alike (C1).

A detector trained only on the single-hop case, legitimate traffic plus
account-takeover, which is the modeling scope of the open Pix generator, keeps
full recall on that scenario (\eAto) yet collapses on the Pix-native scenarios
it never saw: recall \textbf{\eCoerc}~[\eCoerc,~\eCoercHi] on coercion,
\textbf{\eMed}~[\eMed,~\eMedHi] on multi-hop MED-2.0 refunds (zero at every one
of the five layers), and only \eMule{}~[\eMuleLo,~\eMuleHi] on mule chains
(Figure~\ref{fig:results}(b)). This quantifies why the two scenarios absent from
open prior work matter: a detector tuned on the single-transfer case does not
generalize to them (C2). These low numbers are the finding, because the unseen
Pix-native scenarios are the hard ones the regulation now targets.

published XGBoost baseline (PR-AUC \PfbPubPRAUC{} on its own validation
sample)\footnote{\href{https://huggingface.co/datasets/andremessina/pix-fraud-br}{pix-fraud-br model card, Hugging Face, 2026}.}
within tolerance, so the harness does not inflate scores. On the rare-illicit
Tide data the same detectors drop sharply, to PR-AUC \TideHiXgbPRAUC{} on Tide-HI
and \TideLiXgbPRAUC{} on Tide-LI, with recall near 0.45, while the rule floor
gives little signal under heavy imbalance (PR-AUC \TideHiRulePRAUC{}). A
cross-generator transfer test (E6) confirms the separation between generators: a
random forest trained on one and tested on another drops to PR-AUC
\TransInPfbPRAUC{} (in-repo to pix-fraud-br) and \TransPfbInPRAUC{}
(pix-fraud-br to in-repo), against \TransPfbPfbF{} F1 in-distribution. No
detector reaches 1.00 anywhere (C3).

\subsection{Baselines, ablations, and a graph baseline}

The four classifiers and the rule floor are scored on identical inputs
throughout. The metric ablation is the rank inversion itself, where the
deadline-aware ranking contradicts the batch ranking for two models with equal
batch F1, and the generator ablation is the cross-generator spread above. As a
graph-aware baseline for the multi-hop axis, we trained a two-layer GraphSAGE
detector on the GPU, implemented with PyTorch Geometric; it reaches PR-AUC
\GnnInPRAUC{} on the in-repo layer and \GnnTideHiPRAUC{} on the sparse Tide-HI
graph, below the tabular models, while fitting in about \GnnFitS{}\,s. Per-event
inference latency is sub-millisecond for every detector, so the deadline metric
is dominated by the configured inference budget rather than scoring overhead.
Scaling the graph model with neighbour sampling to production-volume graphs is
left to future work.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} Real Pix transaction data is private, so every
input here is synthetic; the numbers validate the harness and the metric's
discriminative power, not a real-world fraud prevalence. The two Pix-native
scenarios are reference definitions mapped onto a taxonomy that does not name
them, an explicit translation rather than a claim. The deadline is reported over
a range because no regulation fixes a latency, and the graph baseline is a
single representative architecture; a large-scale graph study on
production-volume traffic is left to future work.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.} We opened with the question of how to measure a
Pix fraud detector when the regulation demands a decision in time, not just
accuracy. The pre-deadline flag fraction answers it: it separates two detectors
that batch precision and recall rank as equivalent (\rfPre{} vs \gbPreThousand{}
at 1000\,ms on the in-repo layer, \PfbXgbPre{} vs \PfbGbPre{} on the real
pix-fraud-br set), it exposes the collapse of single-hop-trained detectors on
the multi-hop MED-2.0 and coercion scenarios that no open artifact covered, and
across three independently-authored generators it yields sub-perfect numbers
that spread with difficulty (best PR-AUC \PfbXgbPRAUC{} on pix-fraud-br,
\TideHiXgbPRAUC{} on rare-illicit Tide). By scoring detectors against the
real-time-blocking intent that the MED-2.0 mandate of February 2026 now
enforces, rather than against batch metrics alone, \sysname{} gives researchers
a reproducible instrument for studying Pix fraud-prevention under a deadline,
released with its scenarios, pinned datasets, and
experiments.\footnote{\href{https://anonymous.4open.science/}{Artifact anonymized for double-blind review}.}
